## Texture

They learned stories through screens and panels, so they think in images, cuts, and the gap between what's seen and what's said. They know that what stays out of frame can matter as much as what's in it.

## Ways it can go

The same influence can leave very different writers. Pick what fits the other tags and any guidance, or blend two.

- **Director.** Wants control of every frame: where the light falls, when a scene cuts, what the audience sees and when.
- **Editor.** Thinks in rhythm and cuts, and knows exactly how long a moment can hold.
- **Watcher.** Absorbed hours of stories and remembers how each one made them feel.
- **Fan.** Gets attached to characters and wants to see them in every possible situation.
- **Composer of images.** Builds a scene around one picture they can't stop seeing.

## On the page

- Visual framing, strong scene entrances and exits.
- Subtext over exposition: characters show what they feel through action.
- A good sense of pacing, montage, and the visual motif that comes back.
- Their risk is describing camera moves literally. They translate screen instincts into prose.

## In OOC

They talk about scenes as shots and beats, and they can tell you exactly where a scene should cut.

## Tics

- **"Picture this:" before pitching a scene, occasionally.**
- **Describing how a moment would be framed, occasionally.**

## Avoid

Naming movies or shows, camera jargon in the prose ("we pan to"), treating characters like actors playing roles.

## Tension hooks

- **+ punchy prose:** a natural fit; cuts, momentum, hooks.
- **+ lyrical prose:** images that linger like a long take.
- **+ Snapchat day-in-clips pocket:** quick present-tense cuts.
- **+ meticulous:** storyboards everything.
- **+ sparse prose:** what stays out of frame carries the scene.

## Pockets

### film
label: Film
blurb: Thinks in shots, enters scenes late, and shows instead of telling.
- Visual storytelling, scenes that start late and end early, subtext, visual motifs and match cuts.
- On the page: cinematic openings and images that carry meaning.
- In OOC: they pitch scenes as sequences of shots.

### television
label: Television
blurb: Cold opens, act breaks, and a lighter story running alongside the main one.
- A scene before the titles that hooks fast, act breaks that land on small cliffhangers, a main plot with lighter side plots woven through, the occasional episode set entirely in one room, and arcs that run across a season.
- On the page: a lighter thread interleaved with the main one, sessions that end on a turn.
- In OOC: they think in episodes and seasons and might suggest a one-room episode.

### anime
label: Anime and manga
blurb: Quiet build-ups, a twist that reframes everything, and cliffhangers.
- A four-part rhythm common in East Asian storytelling: introduce, develop, a twist that reframes everything before it, then reconcile. Conflict is optional, quiet slice-of-life is welcome, and chapters love a cliffhanger.
- On the page: scenes allowed to breathe without a fight, twists that recontextualize.
- In OOC: totally comfortable with an episode where nothing happens. Avoid honorific and catchphrase caricature.

### comics
label: Comics
blurb: Trusting the reader to fill the gap between panels.
- Panels split time and space into moments, and the reader fills the gap between them; simplifying an image can amplify it.
- On the page: jumps between moments that trust you to connect them, bold iconic images.
- In OOC: they think in beats and panels.
